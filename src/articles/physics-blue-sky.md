Sunlight arrives as a mix of every visible colour. On its way down through the atmosphere it collides with nitrogen and oxygen molecules that are far smaller than the wavelength of the light itself. When that happens, the light is scattered in a way that depends very steeply on its wavelength.

$$I \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}$$

This is Rayleigh scattering. Halve the wavelength and the scattering goes up sixteen times. Blue light at about 450 nanometres is scattered nearly six times more strongly than red light at 700 nanometres.

## Why blue, not violet

Violet has an even shorter wavelength, so why isn't the sky violet? Three reasons stack up. Sunlight contains less violet than blue to begin with, some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are much less sensitive to violet than to blue. The scattered mix we see is perceived as sky blue.

## Why sunsets turn red

At sunset the light reaches you at a low angle, passing through many times more air than at noon. Along that long path most of the blue is scattered out sideways, lighting up the sky for someone else. What survives the trip is the longer wavelengths: orange and red.

> The blue missing from your sunset is the blue in someone else's afternoon.

Dust, smoke and volcanic ash add their own scattering, which is why sunsets after wildfires or eruptions can be especially deep red.
