Every time one of your cells divides, it has to copy its entire genome, two sets of about three billion base pairs each, and hand one complete copy to each daughter cell. There's no master copy kept on file. The molecule's own structure tells the cell how to rebuild it.

## The pairing rule

DNA is two strands wound around each other, joined by pairs of bases. Adenine always pairs with thymine, and cytosine always pairs with guanine. So if you know one strand, you know the other.

During replication, an enzyme called helicase unzips the helix down the middle. Each separated strand becomes a template. DNA polymerase walks along it, reading each base and adding the matching partner to a new strand.

## Half old, half new

The result is two double helices, each made of one original strand and one fresh strand. In 1958, Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl confirmed this by growing bacteria in heavy nitrogen, switching them to normal nitrogen, and measuring the density of their DNA after each round of division. The densities came out exactly as this "semi-conservative" model predicted.

## One strand the awkward way

Polymerase can only build in one direction. On one template that's easy: it follows the unzipping fork continuously. On the other strand it has to work backwards in short bursts, called Okazaki fragments, which are later stitched together.

## Remarkably few typos

Polymerase checks its own work as it goes and removes mismatched bases. A separate repair system catches most of what slips through. Together they bring the error rate down to roughly one mistake per billion bases copied, a handful of new errors in an entire human genome per division.
