For most substances, the solid is denser than the liquid. Melted wax shrinks as it hardens, and a chunk of solid wax sinks in its own melt. Water does the opposite. It expands by about 9 percent when it freezes, so ice is less dense than the liquid around it and floats.

$$\rho_{\text{ice}} \approx 0.917\ \text{g/cm}^3 \qquad \rho_{\text{water}} \approx 1.000\ \text{g/cm}^3$$

## The open lattice

Each water molecule has a slightly negative oxygen end and two slightly positive hydrogen ends. The hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the oxygen of a neighbour, forming a *hydrogen bond*.

In ice, every molecule is locked into hydrogen bonds with four neighbours arranged in a tetrahedron. The geometry builds a hexagonal framework with a lot of empty space inside it. When ice melts, some of those bonds break, the framework partly collapses, and molecules crowd in closer together. The liquid takes up less room.

Liquid water is densest at about 4 °C. Cool it further and it starts forming ice-like clusters, so it expands slightly even before it freezes.

## Why it matters

When a lake cools in winter, the coldest water near freezing rises to the top, and ice forms there first. That floating sheet insulates the water below, which stays liquid. If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, and far less would survive the winter in them.

It's also why roughly 90 percent of an iceberg sits below the waterline: its density is close to, but just under, that of seawater.
