Almost all of your DNA sits in the nucleus of each cell. A small amount doesn't. Inside every mitochondrion, the structure that turns food and oxygen into usable energy, there are a few loops of DNA of its own.

## A guest that never left

The leading explanation is the endosymbiotic theory, championed by biologist Lynn Margulis in 1967. Well over a billion years ago, an ancestral cell took in a bacterium that could use oxygen to release energy. Instead of being digested, the bacterium survived and multiplied inside its host. Over time the two became completely dependent on each other.

## The evidence

Mitochondria still look and behave like bacteria in several ways:

- Their DNA is a small circle, like a bacterial chromosome.
- They have a double membrane, consistent with having been engulfed.
- They divide by splitting in two, as bacteria do, rather than being built from scratch.
- Their ribosomes resemble bacterial ribosomes more than the cell's own.

Gene sequences point to a group called alphaproteobacteria as their closest living relatives.

## A shrinking genome

Human mitochondrial DNA is just 16,569 base pairs long, with 37 genes. Over evolutionary time, most of the original bacterium's genes either disappeared or moved into the nucleus. Why any stayed behind is still debated. One popular idea is that keeping a few genes on site lets each mitochondrion quickly adjust its own energy machinery.

Because the father's mitochondria are almost always destroyed shortly after fertilisation, you inherit yours from your mother. That makes mitochondrial DNA a useful tool for tracing maternal ancestry back through generations.
