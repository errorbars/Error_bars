Atoms bond for the same reason a ball rolls downhill: the bonded arrangement has lower energy than the separate atoms. Forming a bond releases energy, and breaking one costs energy. Everything else is detail about how a particular pair of atoms gets to that lower-energy state.

## Trading electrons

Sodium has a single electron in its outer shell and holds it loosely. Chlorine is one electron short of a full outer shell and pulls hard. Let them meet and the electron moves from sodium to chlorine, leaving a positive sodium ion and a negative chloride ion.

Here's the part that's often skipped. Moving that electron, on its own, actually costs a little energy. The payoff comes afterwards: oppositely charged ions attract strongly, and packed into a crystal of table salt, that attraction releases far more energy than the transfer cost. That's an *ionic bond*.

## Sharing electrons

When two atoms pull on electrons about equally hard, neither can take one from the other. Instead they share a pair, and both nuclei are attracted to the shared electrons between them. That's a *covalent bond*, and it's what holds a hydrogen molecule together.

$$2\,\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

This reaction releases a lot of heat because the bonds in two water molecules are, together, stronger than the bonds in the hydrogen and oxygen you started with.

## A spectrum, not two boxes

How strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons is called its electronegativity. When two atoms differ a lot, you get ionic bonding. When they're identical, the sharing is perfectly even. Most bonds sit somewhere in between: in water, oxygen pulls the shared electrons closer than hydrogen does, which gives the molecule slightly negative and positive ends. That uneven sharing explains much of what makes water unusual.
