Everyone can quote it. Far fewer people can say what it means. The short version: mass and energy are not two different things. They are one quantity, measured in different units, and the conversion factor between them is the speed of light squared.

$$E = mc^2$$

## Where it comes from

In 1905 Albert Einstein published a short follow-up to his paper on special relativity. He imagined a body giving off two equal pulses of light in opposite directions, then looked at the same event from a moving frame. Energy and momentum only balanced if the body had lost a little mass, equal to the energy carried away divided by $c^2$.

That was the surprise. Even sitting perfectly still, an object carries energy simply by having mass. Physicists call this its *rest energy*.

## Why the number is so large

The speed of light is about $3 \times 10^8$ metres per second, so $c^2$ is around $9 \times 10^{16}$. Convert one gram of mass entirely into energy and you get roughly $9 \times 10^{13}$ joules, about the energy released by 21,000 tonnes of TNT.

> A single gram of matter holds about as much energy as a large power station produces in a full day. We just can't usually get it out.

## Where we actually see it

Chemical reactions do change mass, but by around one part in ten billion, far too little to weigh. Nuclear reactions are different. When light nuclei fuse inside the Sun, the product weighs measurably less than the ingredients, and the missing mass leaves as light and heat. The Sun turns roughly four million tonnes of mass into energy every second.

For a moving object, the full relationship includes momentum $p$:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

Set $p = 0$ and you are back to the famous version.
